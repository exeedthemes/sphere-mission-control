WELCOME TO MISSION CONTROL, FLIGHT CREW!

Today you'll step into the shoes of thermal protection engineers. Your mission: keep an astronaut core safely warm against the freezing thermal void of space using smart multi-layer insulation and real-time sensor telemetry.

Your Mission Challenge: Build a waterproof spacesuit capsule for a simulated astronaut ($100\text{ ml}$ of hot water at $80^\circ\text{C}$), submerge it in an ice bath ($0^\circ\text{C}$), and stream live temperature readings to your Mission Control dashboard. You'll compare your real experimental curve against a live computer model (Digital Twin) running Newton's Law of Cooling, and see how closely your hardware matches physics theory!

1. THE SPACE ENVIRONMENT & MISSION OBJECTIVES

Space is one of the most extreme environments humans have ever ventured into. In Low Earth Orbit, spacecraft and astronauts orbiting Earth cycle between violent temperature extremes every $90\text{ minutes}$. In full sunlight, solar radiation bakes spacesuit exteriors up to $+121^\circ\text{C}$. When orbiting into Earth's shadow, ambient temperatures plunge to $-157^\circ\text{C}$ as heat radiates away into deep space.

Without an atmosphere to carry heat away gently, spacesuits rely on specialized Multi-Layer Insulation (MLI). These blankets combine reflective foils that bounce radiant heat back with fluffy spacer materials that stop direct heat conduction.

In this lab, your crew will test different insulation setups to discover how well materials fight heat loss. By logging real sensor telemetry and comparing it with a theoretical cooling curve, you will calculate your crew's Thermal Correlation Score.

Quick Assembly Steps:

  1. Feed the Sensor: Unscrew the dome cap on the PG7 gland and slide the metal temperature probe through the gland body.
  2. Mount on Cap: Push the threaded gland through the hole in the jar cap, and tighten the locknut on the underside so it won't budge.
  3. Tighten the Seal: Hand-tighten the outer dome nut until the rubber ring clamps snugly around the cable. This stops any water from leaking in.
  4. Center the Probe: Make sure the metal sensor tip hangs right in the center of the jar without touching the bottom or sides.

3. THE 4 INSULATION CONFIGURATIONS

Each crew will test one of four insulation setups to see how different materials tackle heat loss:

A. BARE CONTROL k = 0.150

Plain uninsulated jar. Shows how rapidly heat escapes through direct conduction and water currents.

B. COTTON SLEEVE k = 0.080

Fabric wrap. Traps still air pockets within fibers to slow down conductive heat transfer.

C. MYLAR FOIL k = 0.040

Shiny emergency blanket. Reflects radiant thermal infrared waves back toward the hot water.

D. FULL MLI k = 0.015

Multi-layer system combining Cotton + Bubble Wrap + Mylar to block conduction, convection, and radiation all at once!

4. THE PHYSICS: NEWTON'S LAW OF COOLING

Heat always flows from warmer objects to cooler surroundings. Over 300 years ago, Sir Isaac Newton discovered that the rate at which an object cools is proportional to the temperature difference between the object and its environment.

Newton's Law of Cooling (Exponential Decay Model)
$$T(t) = T_{\text{env}} + (T_0 - T_{\text{env}}) \cdot e^{-k \cdot t}$$
Where:
$T(t)$ = Predicted core temperature (°C) at minute $t$
$T_{\text{env}}$ = Ice bath temperature ($0.0^\circ\text{C}$)
$T_0$ = Starting core temperature ($80.0^\circ\text{C}$)
$k$ = Cooling rate constant ($\text{min}^{-1}$)

What does $k$ mean? The cooling constant $k$ measures how easily heat escapes. A smaller $k$ value means superior insulation, a slower temperature drop, and a flatter cooling curve!

7. LAB ANALYSIS & REFLECTION QUESTIONS

Question 1: Why doesn't shiny Mylar foil work by itself in cold water?

Mylar is famous for reflecting heat radiation in space. Why did Mylar alone perform poorly when dipped directly into the ice water bath ($0^\circ\text{C}$) without cotton or bubble wrap underneath?

Question 2: What does the cooling constant ($k$) tell us?

In Newton's Law $T(t) = T_{\text{env}} + (T_0 - T_{\text{env}}) \cdot e^{-kt}$, does a smaller $k$ value mean a better insulator or a worse insulator? Explain using the formula.

Question 3: How does Multi-Layer Insulation (MLI) work together?

Explain how combining Cotton + Bubble Wrap + Mylar in Configuration D creates a team effect that works far better than any single layer alone.

Question 4: Where could experimental errors come from?

List two physical reasons why your real sensor readings might differ from the smooth curve predicted by the computer model.

Question 5: What happens if your ice bath warms up?

If your classroom ice bath warms from $0.0^\circ\text{C}$ to $4.0^\circ\text{C}$ while testing, what should you change in the Mission Control simulator so the prediction stays accurate?

Question 6: What if we double the water volume?

If we doubled the water inside the jar from $100\text{ ml}$ to $200\text{ ml}$, would it cool down faster or slower? Would $k$ increase or decrease?

Question 7: Spacesuit Design Trade-Offs

If adding insulation layers keeps astronauts warmer, why don't space engineers just wrap a spacesuit in $50\text{ layers}$ of MLI? What problems would that cause for an astronaut on a spacewalk?

Question 8: How can you improve your Correlation Score?

Describe two practical things your crew could do to improve your Correlation Score from $75\%$ to over $95\%$ on a second run.

1. CONDUCTION

Heat passing through direct contact as vibrating molecules bump into each other.

2. CONVECTION

Heat carried by moving fluids (liquids or gases) as warm areas rise and cool areas sink.

3. RADIATION

Heat traveling as infrared light waves that can travel right through the vacuum of space.

4. MLI SYSTEM

Multi-Layer Insulation blankets that stop conduction, convection, and radiation together.

5. NEWTON'S LAW

The cooling rule: hot things cool down fast at first, then slow down as they approach room temp.

6. COOLING CONSTANT (k)

How fast an object loses heat. The smaller the k value, the better the insulator!

7. DIGITAL TWIN

A live computer model running math simulations alongside real hardware sensor data.

8. THERMAL SINK

A large surrounding environment (like our 0°C ice bath) that absorbs heat without warming up.

9. HEAT CAPACITY

How much thermal energy a body can store. More water mass means slower cooling!

10. EQUILIBRIUM

The final balanced state reached when the hot core cools down to match the surrounding 0°C bath.
